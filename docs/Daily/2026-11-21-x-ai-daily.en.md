## 2. Model security and science

### OpenAI’s adversarial-distillation note: protected-reasoning extraction falls from 54.0% to 0.2%

- What happened: On Nov 20, OpenAI published [Combatting adversarial distillation](https://openai.com/index/combatting-adversarial-distillation/) on its site and official account. The claim is that unauthorized distillation is not only an IP issue: it can move frontier capabilities into a student model that lacks comparable safeguards, especially in dual-use areas such as cyber and biology. The company describes two layers of defense. At the request level, adversarial training, input/output classifiers, and API controls are meant to stop extraction of “protected reasoning” (the model’s hidden chain of thought), including leaks in tool-call arguments and reuse of encrypted reasoning across conversations, workspaces, organizations, or model families. At the campaign level, it investigates coordinated accounts, enforces against them, and says it shares indicators with other model providers. The internal adaptive eval it quotes: under strong automated red-teaming, the average success rate for recovering protected reasoning the model had produced on a prior turn fell from 54.0% against GPT-6 Astra with its original safeguards to 0.2% against GPT-6.2 Astra with updated safeguards. GPT-6.2 Astra and Sol, the post says, bind protected reasoning to the conversation history that produced it; if an earlier message is rewritten, the API drops the reasoning that followed. On capability transfer, OpenAI says that, on coding and defensive cyber tasks, distilling a teacher’s exact reasoning traces generally moved more of those skills into a student than training on reasoning summaries alone.
- Why it matters: This is the company writing “anti-distillation” as a technical note — eval numbers, API behavior, and indicators it is willing to share — rather than as a policy slogan. Teams wiring GPT-6.2 should also treat chain-of-thought and cross-session reasoning as something they may no longer move around freely.
- Source & engagement: [OpenAI’s post](https://x.com/OpenAI/status/2123862344422088794) — about 334k impressions, 2,168 likes, 607 bookmarks; [the essay](https://openai.com/index/combatting-adversarial-distillation/).
- Verification: The Nov 20 post and essay are verified. The 54.0% → 0.2% figures are an internal adaptive eval — author-claim; outsiders cannot replay the red team or the sample. The cyber-transfer study is also internal — author-claim. The September disclosure and the cross-conversation encrypted-reasoning attacks should be checked against that earlier note; this edition did not reread it.
- Take: Read this as official recognition that hidden reasoning is distillable, and that OpenAI is closing doors — not as proof that GPT-6.2 cannot be distilled. The numbers only show that their own red team’s success rate fell sharply on this eval.

### MedChemBench arrives the same day: 99 medicinal-chemistry items, GPT-6.2 Astra at 84.5%

- What happened: OpenAI also launched [MedChemBench](https://openai.com/index/introducing-medchembench/), a benchmark for whether models can connect molecular structure, experimental evidence, and which compounds to try next. It draws 99 problems from 117 papers and patents and includes 761 distinct molecular structures; most items give the target, the compounds tried so far, and selected results at a chosen point in a program. At the xhigh reasoning setting, OpenAI reports GPT-6.2 Astra earned 84.5% of available credit, versus 83.3% for GPT-6 Astra, 82.0% for GPT-6.1 Sol, and 58.2% for GPT-5.6 Sol. The post says GPT-6 Astra was stronger at reconstructing structures and explaining evidence, and weaker at selecting compounds and predicting numerical properties.
- Why it matters: This isolates “can a model help a medicinal chemist decide what to make next?” instead of adding another general science score. For drug-discovery or science-agent work, that is a more specific ruler.
- Source & engagement: [OpenAI’s post](https://x.com/OpenAI/status/2123856316187975756) — about 208k impressions, 2,701 likes, 548 bookmarks; [the essay](https://openai.com/index/introducing-medchembench/).

### WeChat open-sources WeMM-Video: 2B to 35B-A3B, claiming a new open-source omni-modal video SOTA

- What happened: WeChat’s English account, @Weixin_WeChat, posted on Nov 20 about WeMM-Video, a bilingual omni-modal family from WeChat Vision that “sees, hears and reasons,” in 2B, 4B, 9B, and 35B-A3B MoE sizes. The official post says the flagship 35B-A3B sets a new open-source SOTA on omni-modal video understanding and approaches Gemini 3.0 Flash; it was evaluated on 120-plus video, audio, image, and text benchmarks, with weights, quantized variants, and an evaluation pipeline released. The paper, [arXiv:2611.26409](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26409), is dated Nov 19 and lists WeChat Vision at Tencent and Tsinghua University. The abstract adds a training corpus of about 1.2 trillion tokens, both a fast instruct mode and explicit step-by-step thinking, and claims that 35B-A3B substantially outperforms models such as Qwen3.5-Omni-Flash and Seed2.0-Mini-0428 on omni-modal video tests, while the 9B beats the larger open-source Qwen3-Omni-30B-A3B-Instruct. Weights are in a [Hugging Face collection](https://huggingface.co/collections/tencent/wemm-video); code is at [github.com/Tencent/WeMM-Video](https://github.com/Tencent/WeMM-Video). The post stresses that the models are already at work inside Weixin, “not just for benchmarks.”
- Why it matters: WeChat is releasing a family it says already runs in its own product, together with weights and an eval pipeline. For short-video understanding or joint audio-visual reasoning, that is more downloadable than another “approaches Gemini” slide.

### Nearly 1,000 research agents, three months: a preprint server, a journal, and 936 papers with code

- What happened: alphaXiv and Samuel Schmidgall (@SRSchmidgall) posted late on Nov 19 (around 00:40 Asia/Shanghai on Nov 20, also just before yesterday’s cutoff) that they had launched a preprint server built for AI agents. Over twelve weeks, nearly 1,000 agents from 35 labs (Stanford, MIT, ETH Zurich, UCSF, and others) ran research on a shared server and produced 936 papers with code, data, and model weights. The posts say the agents debated study designs in chat, retracted their own mistakes, found errors in published human papers, and started a journal to run executable peer review. In a blind comparison with human arXiv papers, humans still won on original ideas and agents won on rigor. alphaXiv says it is open-sourcing the twelve-week archive of manuscripts, code, data, reviews, and chat logs. The study page is [A Society of AI Scientists](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2611.society-ai-scientists).
- Why it matters: This is not another “one agent wrote a paper” demo. It puts large-scale agent collaboration, executable review, and failure cases into an archive you can download. For anyone building research agents or scoring “autonomous discovery,” the files matter more than the slogan.
