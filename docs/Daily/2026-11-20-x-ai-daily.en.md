## 2. Compute and policy

### Moonshot reportedly scraps a $150 million Indonesia Blackwell lease on the day the BIS rule takes effect

- What happened: The Information reported on Nov 19, citing people familiar with the matter, that Beijing-based Moonshot AI, the company behind the Kimi models, cancelled a roughly $150 million contract to rent Nvidia Blackwell Ultra B300 capacity in an Indonesian data center through a Southeast Asian neocloud, with service planned as early as next year. The timing sat on the first day in force of a new remote-access rule from the U.S. Commerce Department’s Bureau of Industry and Security: published Nov 16, it appeared in the Federal Register on Nov 19 and took effect the same day. Secondary write-ups say licenses are required to export advanced computing items to Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, and Brazil, and that data centers need a license before supplying more than a set compute threshold to entities headquartered in restricted destinations. The same accounts say Moonshot has already signed a replacement B300 rental with another Southeast Asian cloud provider, still aiming for service next year. Chinese-language posts quickly framed this as a delay to Kimi K4; Moonshot’s official account did not confirm or deny it in this window.
- Why it matters: Export control is no longer only “these chips may not be sold into China.” It now reaches remote rental in third-country data centers, and a named, dollar-sized deal moved on day one.
- Source & engagement: This item is a press report; X posts were mostly low-engagement recaps, so engagement is not used as a heat signal. Primary source: [The Information](https://www.theinformation.com/articles/chinas-moonshot-ai-scraps-150-million-deal-access-nvidia-chips-indonesia)
- Verification: Publication of The Information’s report is verified. The cancelled contract, the replacement lease, and any effect on Kimi K4 training are sourced to anonymous insiders and are unverified by the company. The BIS rule’s publication and effective dates are verified as a matter of public record; the summary of license scope and compute thresholds comes from secondary accounts and should be checked against the Federal Register text.
- Take: Treat this as evidence that the remote-rental path is being closed, not as a confirmed K4 delay. The practical question for other Chinese labs is when replacement capacity shows up, and whether similar contracts are being unwound.

## 3. Open source and developer tools

### Linger: a personal agent that stays on your PC, now past 1,000 GitHub stars

- What happened: Linger, an open-source personal agent from Simplex AI founder Tony Zhang (@zhangboknight), continued to circulate in both English and Chinese feeds. The product pitch is that the model runs locally by default, memory stays on disk, and the agent only reaches files, apps, and the signed-in browser with the user’s say-so; a cloud model can join when the user chooses one. The inference engine, PolyStrata (in the Strata / llama.cpp family), keeps the busiest MoE experts on the GPU and the rest in RAM or on SSD so larger models can run on consumer cards. Official docs repeatedly use this example: a 125B-class model (the docs name Qwen3.8-Flash-Next) on a 12 GB GPU plus 64 GB of RAM. In replies, @zhangboknight also said a 32 GB Mac already reaches “close to Sonnet 4.6, around 50 tokens/s.” @HeyLingerAI said on Nov 19 that the GitHub repo had crossed 1,000 stars. The repo is [simplexai-labs/linger](https://github.com/simplexai-labs/linger); docs live at [docs.heylinger.com](https://docs.heylinger.com/). Zhang’s X bio says he was previously among the earliest core members of @deepseek_ai. A caveat: many near-duplicate promo posts on X had little engagement. The checkable record is the official accounts, the repo, and the docs — not second-hand figures such as “45.5 tok/s on an RTX 3060.”
- Why it matters: This is a concrete attempt to bind local models to a personal agent that has memory and can use apps on the machine, which is more specific than another local chatbot for people who do not want everyday files in the cloud.
- Source & engagement: [Official 1,000-star post](https://x.com/HeyLingerAI/status/2123400819483738277) — about 1,015 impressions, 8 likes; [Zhang on feedback and Discord](https://x.com/zhangboknight/status/2123348135313547324) — about 942 impressions, 16 likes. ## 4. Research

### DeepMind Institute: four paths from AGI to superintelligence

- What happened: On Nov 19 the DeepMind Institute published research scientist Tim Genewein’s essay [What comes after AGI?](https://institute.deepmind.com/essays/what-comes-after-agi), paired with the technical report *From AGI to ASI*. It treats general intelligence as a continuum, with AGI as one point on it. ASI is described as a system that consistently outperforms large teams of human experts on virtually all tasks of human interest, with fewer resources and less time — for example, matching the scientific progress of an entire field in a fraction of the time. The essay lays out four possible routes: continued scaling of models and data, new technological paradigms, large-scale multi-agent coordination, and recursive self-improvement (RSI). It also describes “cognitive industrialization”: if effective compute keeps rising by about an order of magnitude a year, work that costs $1 billion in compute today could fall to around $1,000 in a decade — a trend extrapolation, not a date. Discussion on X was light, but the essay itself is a new official long-form publication.
- Why it matters: It turns “what happens after AGI” into paths and bottlenecks that can be checked against real systems, instead of a single bet on a year.
- Source & engagement: English-language posts were mostly low-engagement recaps. Original: [DeepMind Institute essay](https://institute.deepmind.com/essays/what-comes-after-agi)
- Verification: Publication on Nov 19 on the DeepMind Institute site is verified. The four paths and the cognitive-industrialization figures are research argument and trend extrapolation, author-claim, not observed engineering results.
- Take: The useful part is the path taxonomy and the policy note that restricting one input, such as training compute, may not halt overall progress — not the $1 billion-to-$1,000 line read as a forecast.
